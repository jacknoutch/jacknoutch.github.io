In 2026 I had my first blackberry in June. It was tart, tasty, and on my way to work. I have been looking out each morning at the three or four bramble-weeds in the unused land on one side of my local South London station. I had spotted red under the spiked tendrils during the first heatwave of the year, and in the second I could reliably pick a small handful each morning, for the price of missing the first train.

"Rummle-tummle" is my family's name for apple-and-blackberry crumble, christened when I asked my mother if she would make some more of it, but could only remember some rhythmic phonetics of the original. It was—and perhaps still is—my favourite pudding. And it was made in my family home, in Nottinghamshire, in September.

When I moved my young-man's move to London in late 2015, blackberry-picking remained a seasonal affirmation of who I was. I was happy that even in the metropolis I could find a familiar face and practice. Blackberrying reminded me of my mother, and gave me something I could speak to her about on long-neglected telephone calls. But one distance did grow between our experience—my blackberries grew in early August, or sometimes in July.

The fruit has sometimes been the subject of simple nature poems, dull enough to return to the same images of fingers, pricked and stained, and the lips with juice running down. But picking blackberries is, or should be, too universal an experience to merit publishing beyond a coterie of friends or family. But here I must be wrong. I imagined that every boy and girl learned from their mother that blackberries are freely grown and easy to find in Britain. Yet I have only once in London found others picking at a bush before I did.

One time, I stopped on the way back from my groceries shop at a bush two dozen yards wide, thick with black bounty. My bicycle was propped up on the stand and my bags of shopping bulked the panniers. I had one and a half tupperwares full, when I saw a boy and his mother turn out of the shaded footpath at the end of the bush, on their way to the shop I had not long left. I kept on at my work. The boy scootered ahead of his mother and slowed to watch me, stopping close by, interested with a new idea.

“Would you like one?” I asked. Only after he gave his deliberate “yes” did I realise my error. The boy ate the blackberry and scooted back to his mother. She bent down and spoke to him sternly. As they drew level with me I apologised for presuming and being the stranger offering a young lad something he did not know. She mumbled something I can’t remember.  I was sorry, for the minor corruption. But I was sorrier that  blackberries did not trump stranger danger.

# Tips for picking blackberries

- Bring many tupperwares or other containers.
- Search for brambles among unused, unloved scraps of land. Footpaths may be bordered by them, and they grow plentifully near railway lines and the fast-growing tendrils reach out so far that much of the fruit can be claimed on the safe side of any keep-out fences.
- Look under the leaves, and look at the plant from several directions. Berries like to hide, so move leaves and branches aside to find them.
- A crook is ideal for bringing inaccessible fruit close; bringing the hard-to-reach near is a public service, and allows others to pick the more easily obtained.
- Take one or more friends. They make the process more enjoyable and more plentiful. If you are short, a tall friend will reach the trickier parts. If you are tall, a short person will spot fruit underneath.
- Fruit for later is picked easiest. This is the newly-ripened berry which is entirely black but requires a "pluck" to remove. These are the best for crumbles and may be sweetened. Already-sweet fruit will be soft to the touch and must be more gently prised away.
- Glean, do not strip the plant.
- Return for more another day, if you like.
- Teach passers-by what you know. Teach your children and anyone else's who trusts you with theirs.
- Give and share generously.
